# Answering "what is distinctive about X's treatment of Y"

The user usually knows the doctrine already. The question is what this author does with it that others do not. Distinctiveness is rarely a new conclusion; in confessional traditions it is almost always architecture: which decisions organize the material, which categories are imported from where, who the author argues with, and what is left out. The method below finds that architecture.

## 1. Compression test

Read the author's shortest statements before the monograph: the chapter in their systematic theology, the journal article, the plenary address, the lecture-series titles the book grew from, the publisher's summary. An author with sixteen pages must choose, and the choices are the signature. List the thesis sentences of the compressed text verbatim. Then check which of them the long book merely applies (historical excursuses, polemics, exegesis) and which it adds. Books often originate as a named lecture series (Moore College, Kantzer, Warfield, Stone, Gifford, Hayward, Payton); the lecture titles are the most compressed outline that exists, and lane C should find them.

## 2. Interlocutor map

Count names across the whole text with `scripts/term_counts.py`. Sort into allies (retrieved sources) and foils (targets). The foils define the polemical shape; the proportion of allies from the author's own tradition versus others shows how ecumenical the sourcing is. Note who is conspicuously absent: a reviewer from the absent camp will say so.

## 3. Chronology of the moves

For each candidate distinctive, find its first appearance in the author's earlier articles and books (lane A). A move first built for another locus and later applied is "imported"; a move that first appears in the treatment at hand is "native." Native moves are the strongest candidates for "the author's own." Present this as a small table: move, first built, first applied to the topic.

## 4. Nearest neighbors

Identify the two or three treatments the author is measured against (reviewers will name them; the author's own citations will too). Compare on the exact points of departure, not in general: the same passage, the same term, the same question. A comparison table with one row per feature and one column per author makes the residue visible.

## 5. Reception as a mirror

Reviewers from outside the author's tradition are the best witnesses to what is different, because they name what they resist. Read those first. Then check which of your candidate distinctives no reviewer mentioned; an unremarked distinctive is a gap the user's paper can fill, and worth saying so.

## 6. Vulnerabilities and absences

List what an examiner will press: contested exegesis the argument rests on, imported categories that may not fit the author's other commitments, synthesis presented as argument, questions left indeterminate, interlocutors handled too briskly. Then list what the author does not treat at all. Absences are distinctives.

## 7. The short answer

Write it last and put it first: one paragraph saying whether the distinctiveness is doctrinal or architectural, then a numbered list of the organizing decisions, then one sentence on which of them are native. Everything else in the memo is evidence for that paragraph.
